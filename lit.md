# Democratic Backsliding Project: Literature Review

A large literature estimates the impact of democratization on economic outcomes. The proposed project, to the converse, aims to estimate the effect of democratic backsliding (or autocratic transitions) on economic outcomes.

Below, I summarize the existing literature, focusing on papers that use panel data methods (and ignoring older, simple cross-country regression papers).

### Meyersson (2015)
**Abstract.** *In this paper I examine the development effects of military coups. Whereas previous economic literature has primarily viewed coups as a form of broader political instability, less research has focused on its development consequences independent of the factors making coups more likely. Moreover, previous research tends to group coups together regardless of whether they overthrew autocratic or democratically-elected leaders. I first show that coups overthrowing democraticallyelected leaders imply a very different kind of event than those overthrowing autocratic leaders. These differences relate to the implementation of authoritarian institutions following a coup in a democracy, which I discuss in several case studies. Second, I address the endogeneity of coups by comparing the growth consequences of failed and successful coup as well as matching and panel data methods, which yield similar results. Although coups taking place in already autocratic countries show imprecise and sometimes positive effects on economic growth, in democracies their effects are distinctly detrimental to growth. When overthrowing democratic leaders, coups not only fail to promote economic reforms or stop the occurrence of economic crises, but they also have substantial negative effects across a number of standard growth-related outcomes including health, education, and investment.*

### Madsen, Raschky, and Skali (2015)
**Abstract.** *Using data for political regimes, income and human capital for a sample of 141 countries over the periods 1820–2000 and 1500–2000, this research examines the income and growth effects of democracy when human capital, among other key variables, is controlled for. Linguistic distance-weighted foreign democracy is used as an instrument for domestic democracy. Democracy is found to be a significant determinant of income and growth and the result is robust to various estimation methods and covariates. We find that a one-standard deviation increase in democracy is associated with a 44–98% increase in per capita income.*



### Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo, and Robinson (2019)
**Abstract.**
**Innovation.** First paper to control for GDP dynamics in a sophisticated way and to use a democratization IV. 

**Data.** 175 countries from 1960 to 2010.

**Treatment (democratization) definition.** Papaioannou and Siourounis (2008) binary variable (based primarily on Freedom House and Polity IV, and on other papers' coding when not available).

**Outcome (growth) definition.** Log of GDP per capita from World Bank Development Indicators. In addition, measures of investment, trade (exports plus imports), enrollment in secondary and primary schools,infant mortality, financial flows (net foreign assets over GDP),  total factor productivity (TFP), tax revenues from Hendrix (2010), an index of economic reforms coded by Giuliano et al. (2013), and a measure of social unreast indicating riots and revolts.

**Empirical Strategy.** Several: (1) Regression of log GDP per capita on binary democracy measure, with country and time fixed effects and up to 8 lags of the outcome; (2a) semiparametric outcome-regression based estimate from differencing actual GDP and imputed counterfactual GDP from an outcome model fit using nondemocracy observations; (2b) semiparametric treatment-regression based estimate from propensity score weighting; (2c) a doubly robust estimator combining (2a) and (2b); (3) IV using regional democratization waves (jackknifed average of democracy within countries in the same region that are of the same regime type at the start of the sample, 1960, and lags of this variable).

**Studies autocratic transitions?** Yes; strategy (1) uses both transitions to and away from democracy and treats them symmetrically, strategy (2) is also deployed for reversals from democracy to nondemocracy and presented in Figure A5 (p. 77) ("transitions to nondemocracy produce declines in GDP that are comparable (in absolute value) to the effects on GDP from a transition to democracy"), strategy (3) uses both democratization and autocratization waves to estimate the regression from strategy (1) and likewise treats democratic and autocratic transitions symmetrically.
